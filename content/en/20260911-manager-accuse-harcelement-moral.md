# The day asking for accountability became workplace bullying

I learned last year, somewhat by chance and without having asked, that I was a workplace bully and a toxic manager. I didn't need to take a personality test for that, a simple Monday morning meeting was enough. Since the story has been over for a while and the main people involved have left the company, I can finally tell it calmly.

## The setting

It all starts with the departure of a manager I liked a great deal. While a replacement was being found, I was asked to step in as interim head of a team of three handling fairly varied projects: community management, activations, a bit of content production. I was supported, in theory, by a « digital project manager ». In practice, let's say the title, the salary and the experience listed on the CV didn't quite match what I saw day to day.

So there were four of us, and the results didn't take long. Deadlines were treated as mere suggestions, deliverables went out to the client with spelling mistakes and AI-generated visuals where the characters had six fingers, and every remark was met with « the manager is supposed to protect us ». Protect us from what, I never really found out. From the client, I imagine.

A client sends us their brand guidelines? They're ignored, and I'm told in veiled terms that I'd be better off going back to my web development. The same client sends a rather salty email to the whole team a few days later saying exactly what I had said. I ask a team member with a habit of absences for a justification? I receive it two weeks later, and I get berated during a training session for having dared to ask.

The whole thing cost me about ten kilos, what little hair I had left and, I have to admit, a good part of the regard I had for these people. On the company side, the bill runs to several million CFA francs and clients who left slamming the door.

## One question too many

The breaking point comes on a Saturday morning, the day of a big activation for a client. Two members of the team (the other two having already vanished) run a live stream of frankly mediocre quality. And to top it all off, one of the two « forgets » to hand the winners their prizes, on the grounds that they had arrived late.

On Sunday, the client calls me, furious. I spend my day putting out the fire with all the diplomacy I can muster. On Monday, I call in the person concerned and ask her, still politely, what happened. No shouting, no overturned desk. Facts, figures, and a fairly simple question: « why? ».

The answer lands, and I hadn't seen it coming: « This is disparagement, this is a campaign of workplace bullying. » It should be said that the day before, I'd already been treated to a similar speech over WhatsApp, after expressing my displeasure about another blunder by the same person, one that had lost the company an important client a few months earlier.

I understood that day that the management software had received an update I had missed. Losing money is a minor episode, delivering rubbish is part of the creative process, and asking for accountability is an assault.

## How to stay a « cool » manager when everything is sinking

Since high standards have apparently become a pathology, I told myself I had to adapt. So here, for good measure, are the few rules I should have followed to remain a good guy in the eyes of my team.

### Rule number 1: look elsewhere

If a project is sinking, don't look at the project. The sky is magnificent this time of year, and its reflection on the Ébrié lagoon is very soothing.

### Rule number 2: celebrate departures

A client leaves screaming? Organise a little party to celebrate this « refocusing opportunity ». There will be bissap juice.

### Rule number 3: smile

Money is evaporating? Smile, stress is bad for the complexion, and profitability is a very 20th-century notion anyway. Compensate instead by gaining weight and losing your hair, as I did. It's a small sacrifice to remain kind.

In short, stop asking questions. Become a manager who sees nothing, hears nothing and says nothing. It may be the end of your company, but at least nobody will complain about your toxicity while the ship goes down.

## What I did instead

Unfortunately for my image, I didn't manage to apply these rules. I accepted being the bad guy, I kept asking for accountability and I said what I thought of the deliverables. And I'm still here. So is the company, along with the clients who survived the episode. Things have even improved, slowly but surely.

The people concerned, for their part, have mostly left. Of the two who remained after the storm, one resigned shortly after coming under the interim management of someone deemed more « cool ». As for the other, I have no idea, and frankly it's no longer my problem.

Today, I'm back in my natural habitat, which is development, where the logic of algorithms is noticeably less capricious than that of certain humans. The developers currently under my responsibility seem, by the way, to be getting along very well with my toxic personality: they work, they improve and they deliver. It would seem that clearly saying what you expect from people is still the most honest way of respecting them.

And you, have you ever been « the bad guy » simply for expecting work done properly? We can discuss it in the comments, calmly, I wouldn't want to bully anyone 🙂
