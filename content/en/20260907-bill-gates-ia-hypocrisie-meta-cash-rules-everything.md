# Bill Gates wants to slow down AI: hypocrisy is cheap once you've already cashed in

In late August, Bill Gates published [a 6,000-word memo on artificial intelligence](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/26/bill-gates-sounds-the-alarm-on-an-ai-transition). The tone surprised everyone. Tech's eternal optimist is sounding the alarm: AI is moving faster than governments, it will shake up white-collar and blue-collar workers alike, it opens up unprecedented cyber and biological risks, and « there is no plan for entering the AI era ».

His line, quoted everywhere: AI will be « the greatest equalizer ever invented, or the worst source of injustice ».

His remedies? A tax on AI tokens, a tax on robots that replace workers, and the money collected used to fund retraining and safety nets. In short, slow down, regulate, redistribute.

On the substance, I can't say he's wrong, and I say this without irony: the memo is useful. When a man of that stature explains that the transition to AI will be « one of the most turbulent periods in human history », decision-makers listen, even in Abidjan. That's something.

But allow me to cough a little.

## The track record

A quick reminder for the younger readers. Bill Gates is not a philosopher who discovered tech late in life. He is the man who, as early as 1976, wrote his famous « open letter to hobbyists » to explain to tinkerers that sharing code was theft.

He is the boss of the Microsoft of the 90s and 2000s, the one behind the « embrace, extend, extinguish » doctrine: adopt an open standard, extend it with proprietary bits, then smother the competition. The one behind the « Halloween documents », those internal memos that treated Linux as a threat to be neutralised. The one convicted of abusing its dominant position on both sides of the Atlantic.

He is also the man who, in 2005, called the defenders of free culture « modern-day communists », while his right-hand man Steve Ballmer was comparing Linux to a cancer.

For thirty years, anything that looked like sharing, like openness, like a technology you couldn't bill per unit, was fought by this man and his company with remarkable energy. And here he is today as guardian of the common good, worried that technology is going too fast, too far, without guardrails, in the hands of a few overly powerful companies.

## Hypocritical, yes. So what?

Let's be honest, it is a somewhat hypocritical position. The word is strong, but it is accurate. Gates is calling for brakes on a technological revolution after being the main beneficiary of the previous one, which he led without the slightest brake.

What's more, Microsoft, of which he remains one of the biggest shareholders, is one of OpenAI's main backers and sells AI by the shovelful through Azure. It's easier to propose a tax on tokens when you've already cashed in your own.

But we'll have to get used to it, because this is the normal cycle of tech fortunes. First you break everything, crush the competition and ignore the critics. Then you cash in. And finally you become wise: you create a foundation, you write memos, you worry about humanity.

This isn't specific to Gates. It's the trajectory of every industrial baron since Carnegie and Rockefeller. The repentant always has more credibility than the victim, because he has the means to pay for the microphones.

In his defence, Gates at least has the decency to wait until retirement. Others do their conversion while still in office.

## Meta, or the live conversion

In January 2025, Mark Zuckerberg announced his great return to « free speech »: the end of fact-checking in the United States, lighter moderation, a mea culpa over the « censorship » of previous years. At the same time, the company was repainting itself as the champion of « open » AI with its Llama models, distributed for free. The closed giant of Facebook turned apostle of openness. Does that ring a bell?

Then the courts came through.

In March 2026, a New Mexico jury ordered Meta to pay 375 million dollars for 37,500 deliberate violations of the unfair trade practices law. The jury found that the company had knowingly harmed children's mental health and concealed what it knew about the sexual exploitation of minors on its platforms. In August, the same court raised [the total bill to 567 million dollars](https://www.noovo.info/nouvelles/justice/article/mineurs-sur-les-reseaux-sociaux-un-tribunal-condamne-meta-a-verser-567-m/), with corrective measures that go to the heart of the product: screen time limits, restrictions on notifications, protection against certain adult contacts, age verification.

In other words, what researchers, parents and a few employees had been saying for ten years was found to be true. Meta knew, and Meta chose not to know.

And while a court was listing the damage, the company was telling the world it was defending freedom and openness. Meta's conversion, like Gates's, costs nothing. It comes after the cashing in, and it even serves to protect the next round.

It's what I was already writing [about the « yes to excision » campaign](/en/20260822-oui-a-lexcision-non-diversion-rhetorique): platforms don't distinguish approval from anger, they see engagement and they push. The excesses are not accidents of the model, they are the model. A New Mexico jury has just said so with numbers.

## What to take from this

You can applaud the substance of Gates's memo without buying the posture. Taxing tokens, funding retraining, slowing the flight away from human labour: these are real avenues, and they deserve to be discussed here too, where AI is going to hit economies that have no safety nets at all.

You do have to remember who is speaking, though. Today's sage is yesterday's predator, and his memo will change nothing about what Microsoft does tomorrow. Listen to the argument, and keep an eye on the author.

And don't expect it to change. The late conversions of the powerful are not hypocrisy in the moral sense, they are wealth management. You protect what you've earned by regulating those who come after.

## Conclusion

The great philosopher Clifford « Method Man » Smith, of the Wu-Tang Clan, had already summed it all up in 1993 with an economy of words Gates will never reach in 6,000: Cash Rules Everything Around Me.

C.R.E.A.M.

Gates is right about AI. Meta was found liable for its networks. And in both cases, the conclusion is the same: in the end, money fixes everything. It pays for the damage, it pays for the memos, it even pays for the wisdom.

Get the money. Dollar dollar bill, y'all.
