In late August, Bill Gates published [a 6,000-word memo on artificial intelligence](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/26/bill-gates-sounds-the-alarm-on-an-ai-transition). The tone surprised everyone. Tech's eternal optimist is sounding the alarm and writes that AI is moving faster than governments, that it will shake up white-collar and blue-collar workers alike, that it opens up unprecedented [cyber and biological risks](/en/20260926-apocalypse-ia-nucleaire-distributeurs-internet), and that "there is no plan for entering the AI era".

His line, quoted everywhere, says AI will be "the greatest equalizer ever invented, or the worst source of injustice".

As remedies, he proposes a tax on AI tokens, a tax on robots that replace workers, and using the money collected to fund retraining and safety nets.

On the substance, I can't say he's wrong, and I say this without irony, the memo is useful. When a man of that stature explains that the transition to AI will be "one of the most turbulent periods in human history", decision-makers listen, all the way to Abidjan. That's something.

But I have a reservation about the man writing it.

## The track record

For the younger readers, Bill Gates is the man who, as early as 1976, wrote his famous "open letter to hobbyists" to explain to tinkerers that sharing code was theft.

He is the boss of the Microsoft of the 90s and 2000s, the one behind the "embrace, extend, extinguish" doctrine, which means adopting an open standard, extending it with proprietary bits, then smothering the competition. It is also the Microsoft of the "Halloween documents", those internal memos that treated Linux as a threat to be neutralised, and the one convicted of abusing its dominant position on both sides of the Atlantic.

He is also the man who, in 2005, called the defenders of free culture "modern-day communists", while his right-hand man Steve Ballmer was comparing Linux to a cancer.

For thirty years, anything that looked like sharing, like openness, like a technology you couldn't bill per unit, was fought by this man and his company with remarkable energy. And here he is today defending the common good, worried that technology is going too fast, too far, without guardrails, in the hands of a few overly powerful companies.

## A hypocritical position, and a common one

I find this position hypocritical. It's a strong word, but Gates is calling for brakes on a technological revolution after being the main beneficiary of the previous one, which he led without the slightest brake.

What's more, Microsoft, of which he remains one of the biggest shareholders, is one of OpenAI's main backers and sells AI in bulk through Azure. It's easier to propose a tax on tokens when you've already cashed in your own.

But we'll have to get used to it, because this is the normal cycle of tech fortunes. First you break everything, crush the competition and ignore the critics, then you cash in, and finally you become wise, you create a foundation, you write memos and you worry about humanity.

It's the trajectory of every industrial baron since Carnegie and Rockefeller. The reformed sinner always has more credibility than the victim, because he can afford to make himself heard.

In his defence, Gates at least has the decency to wait until retirement. Others convert while still in charge.

## Meta's conversion while still in charge

In January 2025, Mark Zuckerberg announced his great return to ["free speech"](/en/20260907-free-speech-oui-mais-pas-ca), with the end of fact-checking in the United States, lighter moderation and a mea culpa over the "censorship" of previous years. At the same time, the company was presenting itself as the champion of "open" AI with its Llama models, distributed for free. The closed giant of Facebook was now defending openness too, like Gates today.

Then the courts stepped in.

In March 2026, a New Mexico jury ordered Meta to pay 375 million dollars for 37,500 deliberate violations of the unfair trade practices law. The jury found that the company had knowingly harmed children's mental health and concealed what it knew about the sexual exploitation of minors on its platforms. In August, in the second phase of the trial, the same court ordered Meta to pay [a further 567 million dollars](https://www.noovo.info/nouvelles/justice/article/mineurs-sur-les-reseaux-sociaux-un-tribunal-condamne-meta-a-verser-567-m/), bringing the total to more than 940 million, with corrective measures that go to the heart of the product: screen time limits, restrictions on notifications, protection against certain adult contacts, age verification.

So what researchers, parents and a few employees had been saying for ten years was found to be true. Meta knew and concealed it.

And while a court was listing the damage, the company was telling the world it was defending freedom and openness. Meta's conversion, like Gates's, costs nothing, because it comes after the cashing in and it serves to protect the next round.

I already wrote [about the "yes to FGM" campaign](/en/20260822-oui-a-lexcision-non-diversion-rhetorique) that platforms don't distinguish approval from anger, that they see engagement and they push. The excesses are part of the model, and a New Mexico jury has just said so with numbers.

## What to take from Bill Gates's memo

You can applaud the substance of Gates's memo without buying the posture. Taxing tokens, funding retraining and slowing the flight away from human labour are serious avenues, and they deserve to be discussed here too, where AI is going to hit economies that have no safety nets at all.

You do have to remember who is speaking, though. The man calling for caution today was crushing his competitors yesterday, and his memo will change nothing about what Microsoft does tomorrow. Listen to the argument, and keep an eye on the author.

And don't expect it to change. The late conversions of the powerful are a form of wealth management, since you protect what you've earned by regulating those who come after.

## Cash Rules Everything Around Me

The great philosopher Clifford "Method Man" Smith, of the Wu-Tang Clan, had already summed it all up in 1993 with an economy of words Gates will never reach in 6,000: Cash Rules Everything Around Me.

C.R.E.A.M.

Gates is right about AI and Meta was found liable for its networks, but in both cases money fixes everything, since it pays for the damage, the memos and the wisdom.

Get the money. Dollar dollar bill, y'all.
