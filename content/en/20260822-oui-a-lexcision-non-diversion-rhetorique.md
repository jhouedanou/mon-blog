This blog is about technology and digital culture, so the title may come as a surprise. Stay anyway, because this "yes to FGM" campaign is a pure product of the platforms and we're right on topic.

A "yes to FGM" campaign has been circulating on Facebook and Twitter out of Mali. Let me say it again: **A "yes to FGM" campaign has been circulating on Facebook and Twitter out of Mali.**


Because that's where some of us, Africans online, have got to in 2026. We no longer discuss what is being done, we discuss words, context, sensitivities, traditions, and while we debate, the practice quietly carries on. And it's not limited to FGM, oh no… there are plenty of examples.

## Three examples of normalisation

### The euphemism

Change the word, and the problem disappears. In the country where I live, online prostitution has become "gérer bizi" (read: "running a business"), a fun and lucrative activity, apparently. Nobody talks about the consequences, which only worry those they happen to.

### Polarisation

Turn a question into a fight between two camps, and it becomes impossible to state a fact without being filed on one side. On the shores of the Ébrié lagoon, the "red pill" community and its pseudo-feminist counterpart feed the permanent clash between the sexes rather than the equality they claim to defend. These people claim to know everything about love, and spend their time taking it apart.

To make enemies for life, all you have to do is say what our parents showed us, that relationships demand sacrifices on both sides and above all that, in a relationship, you have to be friends before being lovers. So, "young kings", "young queens", your rants and your conclusions interest nobody but you, and it shows that you're each more miserable than the last.

### Making a spectacle of it

In French-speaking Africa, on "Meta", [slander and harassment](/en/20250115-l-histoire-danne) go by the names of "gbarai", "buzz", "soko", "kongossa", all of it served up by "bloggers" to their fans, all the time, without a glance at the people targeted, as if social media were [a grin](/en/20250605-facebook-nest-pas-un-grin), the street-corner tea circle. Prison stops nobody either, since all you have to do is "ask for forgiveness".

Euphemism, polarisation and spectacle are three variants of the same move, which is shifting the debate from the victim to the words. As long as it stays bizi and gbarai, we can shrug.

## To each their own feed

That's what I do, [glass of water in hand](/en/20241022-restons-zen). Everyone is free to spend their bandwidth however they like, and I've been hiding this kind of content for years.

Facebook's algorithm got the message.

Twitter's, much less so, and maybe that's why I stumbled, one evening, on a free-for-all between those who say "yes to FGM" and **those who have a brain.**

Excuse me, I meant those who are against it, as we all should be.

Excuse my bias too, but serious subjects turned into ideological ones, no thanks. And this time, we've left the clash between bored adults for a rhetorical diversion on a subject that is anything but trivial.

## The casus belli from Mali

The controversy, [documented by BBC Afrique](https://www.bbc.com/afrique/articles/c4g3nr40drwo), started in Mali in response to a mobilisation against female genital mutilation. On Facebook and Twitter, men, and more surprisingly women, calmly explain that we should keep on cutting young girls.

Or rather little girls, to "build their future" and protect them from relationships outside marriage.

The logic is that we don't trust a girl's judgement, so we remove part of her body on her behalf, all in the name of African values and a supposed religious prescription.

The most extraordinary part is their slogan, "We want to protect her", flanked by a logo in the shape of a razor blade. I thought it was a parody, but it isn't.

Proof in pictures.

## NO, with no nuance

Faced with this, there is only one possible position, and it's NO.

* We don't deconstruct.
* We don't debate.
* No "yes, but".
* No "to each their own culture".
* No "respect for sensitivities and religions".
* No "you have to understand the context".

N.O.

You do not mutilate a child's body, and that limit is absolute. These are girls who asked for nothing, who consented to nothing, and from whom adults remove part of their anatomy in the most barbaric way.

## What algorithms make of "yes to FGM"

These people are not mad. There's a logic behind their posts, and it comes from the platforms far more than from FGM.

They occupy a position more than they hold a conviction. On a social network, an opinion is worth its rarity, whether it's correct or not, and since everyone is against FGM, saying "I'm against it" earns nothing. 

Saying the opposite puts you alone against everyone, and therefore visible, and that visibility pays.

All of this runs on outrage, because the algorithm doesn't tell approval from anger, it sees engagement and it pushes. Every "are you sick or what?" grows, for free, the audience of the person it insults. That's why these accounts never try to convince anyone, since convincing would end the conversation and the conversation is what pays them.

The sovereigntist talk serves as justification. Defending FGM lets you present yourself as a defender of authentic Africa against the West, except that the first people fighting FGM in Africa are African, often survivors. Painting them as foreign mouthpieces means deciding for them a second time.

And above all, there's the feeling that it costs nothing. Behind a handle or a profile, everyone is convinced that posting has no consequences and that words weigh nothing there.

## Enough

So what we have is a mutilation encouraged online for a single benefit, followers.

That is the economy of "yes to FGM", people trading the pain of children they don't know for visibility they won't keep. It's commerce far more than ideology.

There's neither a side to spare nor a nuance to find here. There are children, a blade, and adults deciding on their behalf in front of an audience.

Enough.
