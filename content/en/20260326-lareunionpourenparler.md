# The "let's talk about it" meeting

In my line of work, there is a disease.

An endemic one.

No, not the "crisis meeting", nor the "kickoff".

That one only shows up when the waves of ego and lack of arguments, carefully cultivated by an education system that rewards regurgitation, come crashing against the wall of technical reality.

## The "let's talk about it" meeting.

It always starts the same way: a request made with the nonchalance of someone who will not have to carry it out.
Dropped between two messages, as if the effort in time and resources it represents simply did not exist. A request whose size is inversely proportional to the confidence with which it is made.

In response, you send a technical analysis: options compared, risks anticipated, recommendations for tools that already do the job, a detailed development plan. In other words, you do the thinking for the person who did not take the time to do it.

The reply comes back immediately, and vaguely.
*"I don't really see it. I'm not comfortable with this."*

Followed by the ritual proposal:
*"Can we do a call to talk about it?"*
Which, translated, means:

*"I have no counter-argument. Just a conviction, naked, floating above the thread like a sovereign decree."*

The real problem runs deeper than a colleague's ego or a company's culture.

It is structural.

Our education system, in French-speaking Africa, in France, or anywhere else in the sphere inherited from the French model, does not train people for adversarial thinking and pragmatism, unlike the Anglo-Saxon world.

It trains them to regurgitate.

You learn to recite, to validate, to tick boxes. The person who gives the right answer gets rewarded, not the one who asks the best question. Debate is not a skill you develop, it is a threat you learn to dodge.

The useless meeting is a consequence of this lack of training.
- Nobody learned how to formulate a rigorous objection in front of their teachers.
- Nobody learned to tell an intuition from an argument.

So a meeting gets called, because the meeting, at least, gives the illusion of seriousness.\
Without requiring any of its substance.

## What should you do?

At that moment, you have to avoid falling into the trap of yet another ghost meeting, because you have already proposed a reasoned and documented solution. Nothing productive will come out of that meeting, I guarantee it.
Be pragmatic, and ask the other person to put their concerns in writing. That is a healthy basis for continuing the conversation.

## And did it work in your case?

Yes, but only by insisting... in many (too many) cases, I got one of these replies:

"We'll see."
"It's not that urgent."
"I'm busy right now."

In those cases, keep insisting, not to humiliate anyone, but to help the person articulate what is bothering them and turn vague ideas into real objections. And if, despite your efforts, the written answer never comes, or comes back empty, then no meeting, because a useful meeting starts with a useful sentence.

PS: If you decide to hold that meeting anyway, plan an agenda, a time limit (10 minutes max, no lateness tolerated) and a single question to settle.

With arguments.
